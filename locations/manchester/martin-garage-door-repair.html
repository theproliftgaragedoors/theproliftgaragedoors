Single-Car vs. Two-Car Garage Doors in Martin

Because Martin has a genuine mix of older single-car garages and newer two-car garages, homeowners often ask how the two differ when it comes to repair. Here's the short version:

  • Spring sizing. A two-car door needs springs rated for roughly double the weight and width of a single-car door, so parts are not interchangeable between the two.
  • Opener strength. Wider doors typically pair with a stronger opener motor; an underpowered opener on a two-car door tends to strain, run hot, or fail early.
  • Balance sensitivity. A two-car door shows uneven spring tension more visibly — one side lifting before the other — because the panel spans a wider opening.
  • Hardware age. Single-car doors in Martin's older split-levels and ranches are more likely to still have original hinges, rollers, and sometimes extension springs.
  • HOA and subdivision guidelines. Homes in developments like Line Street Estates may fall under association guidelines for door style or color — worth checking before ordering a replacement.
  • Repair cost. Repairs on two-car doors generally involve larger, more expensive parts than the same repair on a single-car door; a technician can confirm the difference for your specific door.

What Do the Blinking Lights on My Garage Door Opener Mean?

A blinking light on the opener motor or wall control is the system's way of flagging a fault it detected. The exact blink count and what it corresponds to varies by opener brand and model — check the label inside the motor housing or your manual for the precise code — but in general, a blinking opener light points to one of these categories:

  • Misaligned or obstructed safety sensor. The most common cause. The two sensors near the bottom of the tracks need a clear, level line of sight to each other; dust, a spider web, or a nudged bracket is often enough to trigger it.
  • Travel limit out of adjustment. The opener doesn't recognize where "fully open" or "fully closed" should be, often after a power outage or a manual reset.
  • Force setting too high or too low. The opener senses resistance it isn't configured to expect, which can also be a symptom of a spring losing tension rather than the opener itself failing — more common on heavier two-car doors.
  • Worn drive component. A stripped gear, worn belt, or slipping chain can trigger a fault code even though the motor itself still runs.
  • Wiring or circuit board issue. Less common, but a loose connection or aging logic board can produce an intermittent blink with no obvious mechanical cause.

If clearing an obvious sensor obstruction and realigning the sensors doesn't stop the blinking, call (844) 901-6101 — a technician can read the specific code for your opener brand and fix the underlying cause rather than just resetting the symptom.

Signs Your Martin Garage Door Needs a Technician

Most garage door failures don't happen without warning. Catching these signs early, whether you have an older single-car door or a newer two-car door, usually means a smaller repair instead of an emergency call:

  • The door is noticeably louder than it used to be, especially a grinding, popping, or scraping sound during travel.
  • One side lifts before the other, a sign of uneven spring tension or a cable starting to fray — more visible on wide two-car doors.
  • The opener runs but the door doesn't move, often pointing to a broken spring or disconnected trolley.
  • Visible gaps or daylight around the bottom seal or side weatherstripping.
  • The door reverses without an obstruction, usually a sensor alignment issue.
  • Rust or fraying is visible on the cables or spring coils.
  • The door feels heavier to lift by hand than it used to, a sign the springs are losing tension.
  • Panels look bowed or the door doesn't sit flush when fully closed.

What's the difference between torsion springs and extension springs?

Torsion springs mount horizontally above the door opening and twist to store energy; they're standard on most doors built since the 1990s, including newer two-car doors in Martin's subdivisions, and generally last longer and operate more smoothly. Extension springs run along the horizontal tracks on either side of the door and stretch to store energy; they're more common on older single-car garages and typically need safety cables as a backup. Both fail the same way — under high tension — and both should only be replaced by a trained technician.
